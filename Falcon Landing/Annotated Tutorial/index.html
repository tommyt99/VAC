1. Why this is hard

An airplane landing converts kinetic energy into lift, drag, and rolling friction. The vehicle is already the right way up. A Falcon 9 first stage arriving at a pad is a slender, almost-empty tube falling engines-first toward a concrete disk. No wings. Grid fins are control surfaces, not a lifting body. Nine Merlins were sized for a 549-tonne stack, not for hovering ~25 t of dry booster. The landing burn is a late commit: light too late and you hit; light too early and the engine, which cannot throttle to weight, lofts you back into the air.

Mechanically it is an inverted pendulum with the hinge at the engine gimbal and the mass above it. Open-loop upright thrust does not stabilize it. You must keep the thrust vector under the center of mass, or the horizontal acceleration from any lean integrates twice into a miss, then a tip-over.

The trainer you already have is that plant with most of the clothing stripped off. It starts at terminal descent — not MECO, not boostback. The chips below map the essay onto that file, not onto a fictional GNC stack.

3. The inverted pendulum

This is the conceptual core, and it is what the trainer actually integrates.

3.1 States and geometry

Work in a plane. Origin at the intended touchdown point, $+x$ horizontal, $+z$ up. The engine gimbal sits a distance $\ell$ below the center of mass. $\theta$ is body angle from vertical, positive when the nose leans toward $+x$. $\delta$ is gimbal angle, positive when the thrust vector rotates from the body axis toward $+x$.

Seven states:

$$ \mathbf{s} = \bigl(x,\; z,\; v_x,\; v_z,\; \theta,\; \omega,\; m\bigr) $$

In the file that object is literally s = {x, z, vx, vz, theta, omega, m}. Controls are $T(t)=u\cdot T_{\max}$ and $\delta(t)$, both bounded.

%%{init: {'theme':'dark'}}%%
flowchart TB
  N["Interstage / grid fins"] --- COM((CM))

  G --> Tx["Tx = T sin(theta+delta)"]
  G --> Tz["Tz = T cos(theta+delta)"]
  COM --> W["mg"]
  G --> M["M = ell T sin(delta)"]

Figure 2. Planar free-body. Thrust is applied at the gimbal; weight at the CM; moment is r cross T.

COM height is not a constant. Fuel fraction walks $\ell$ from $0.40L$ (wet landing load) to $0.45L$ (dry):

$$ \ell(m) = L\bigl(0.45 - 0.05\,\mathrm{frac}\bigr),\quad \mathrm{frac}=\mathrm{clamp}\bigl((m-m_{\mathrm{dry}})/m_{\mathrm{fuel0}},\,0,1\bigr) $$

Inertia is a rod of length $L$ about a COM that is not at $L/2$: $I = m\bigl(L^2/12 + (L/2-\ell)^2\bigr)$. Atmosphere is a one-line exponential, $\rho = 1.225\,\exp(-z/8500)$.

3.2 Translation and the moment $M=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{T}$

Thrust points out the nose when $\delta=0$. In components:

$$ \begin{aligned} T_x &= T\sin(\theta+\delta) \\ T_z &= T\cos(\theta+\delta). \end{aligned} $$

Newton, with gravity and an optional aero force:

$$ \begin{aligned} m\dot v_x &= T\sin(\theta+\delta) + F_{a,x} \\ m\dot v_z &= T\cos(\theta+\delta) - mg + F_{a,z} \\ \dot m &= -\frac{T}{I_{\mathrm{sp}}\,g_0}. \end{aligned} $$

The gimbal is at $\mathbf{r}_{G/\mathrm{cm}}=\ell(-\sin\theta,\,-\cos\theta)$. The 2-D moment about the CM collapses to

$$ M = \ell T\sin\delta $$

The essay writes $I\dot\omega=-\ell T\sin\delta$ from a geometry sign on $+\delta$. The sim uses M = hc * T * Math.sin(delta) then omega += (M/I)*dt. Same lever; the sign is absorbed when the PD later inverts the gimbal. For small $\delta$, $\sin\delta\approx\delta$ and $I\dot\omega \approx \ell T\,\delta$ in the sim’s convention.

Gimbal is not a pure attitude effector. The same $\delta$ that makes a moment also steals a slice of $T$ into the horizontal channel — which is why stepPhysics applies $T$ at the tail, not as a couple plus a force at the CM.

3.3 Why open-loop upright thrust is unstable

Park at hover: $T=mg$, $\theta=0$, $\delta=0$. Linearize:

$$ \ddot x = g\,(\theta + \delta), \qquad \ddot\theta = -\frac{mg\ell}{I}\,\delta. $$

Implied, not coded. There is no linearized plant object. Freeze deltaCmd = 0 in the living sim with a small s.theta and you will watch $x$ run away while $\theta$ sits — the sanity check in the trainer README. Constrain the tail to $x=0$ and the poles sit at $\pm\sqrt{g/\ell}$.

A 1° lean at $g$ is about $0.17\,\mathrm{m/s}^2$. In ten seconds that is 8.5 m of position. You have left the pad. Adding the task “keep the engine over the X” produces the broomstick:

$$ \ddot\theta - \frac{g}{\ell}\theta = 0. $$

For $\ell$ of order 17–19 m (the sim’s hCom), the unstable time constant is about 1.3 s.

3.4 Attitude command from desired acceleration

Guidance asks for a vector acceleration. The inner loop should point the rocket so that

$$ \frac{T}{m}\,\hat e_T - g\hat z \approx \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{cmd}}, \qquad \hat e_T \parallel \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{cmd}} + g\hat z. $$

The closed form in the plane is $\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}=\mathrm{atan2}(a_{x,\mathrm{cmd}},\,a_{z,\mathrm{cmd}}+g)$. The trainer implements the same idea as a small commanded tilt that kills $v_x$ and walks $x$ back, then inverts $M=\ell T\sin\delta$ for $\delta$:

$$ \theta_{\mathrm{cmd}} = \mathrm{clamp}(-k_v v_x - k_x x), \qquad \delta = \frac{I\,\alpha_{\mathrm{des}}}{\ell\,T} $$

4. Vertical channel / suicide burn

Ignore attitude for a page. The vertical channel is a double integrator with a bounded, strictly positive acceleration. That bound is why SpaceX does not hover the last kilometer.

4.1 T/W and the throttle floor

Official Merlin 1D sea-level thrust is 845 kN. The User’s Guide throttle band is 190,000 → 108,300 lbf (~57%). Unofficial dry mass ~25,600 kg. At that mass, official minimum thrust is still T/W $\approx 1.9$. One Merlin at minimum throttle still lifts an almost-empty booster. You cannot hover. You can only pass through hover.

The trainer is honest about the high T/W and dishonest about the floor: $u\in[0,1]$ with no $T_{\min}$. Dry T/W is $T_{\max}/(m_{\mathrm{dry}}g)\approx 3.37$. That is why a late light or a stuck-high throttle is unsurvivable, and why the hover-slam exists.

4.2 Constant-thrust ignition altitude

Double integrator, downward speed $V=-v_z>0$ at height $h$, constant $a=T/m>g$, want $(z,v_z)=(0,0)$:

$$ h_\star = \frac{V^2}{2(a-g)}. $$

The sim is that formula plus three honest fudges, not a different theory: leftover $v_x$ and $|x|$, a 1.06 scale, and +12 m of pad so the last tens of meters can throttle down. $V_{\mathrm{land}}=-1.3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ sits in the numerator so the target is a walking-pace slam, not a dead stop in the air. $V\to$ vDown = max(-s.vz, 0), $a-g\to$ aMaxNet.

Coast until $h\le h_{\mathrm{sb}}$, then slam. If the burn kills speed well above the deck, cut and fall — do not hover at 150 m.

$$ u = 0 \text{ until } h \le h_{\mathrm{sb}},\quad \text{then solve } a_{\mathrm{net}} = (v_z^2 - V_{\mathrm{land}}^2)/(2h) $$

4.3 Dead time — and why they do not hover the last kilometer

Real ignition is not a step. Chilldown, TEA-TEB, turbopump spin-up, and thrust rise eat time. Guidance must fire early by that dead time, or $h_\star$ is a lie.

Not in the 2D trainer. $T$ is an instantaneous knob. There is no first-order lag and no $T_{\min}$. The hover-slam still exists because dry T/W $\gg 1$: the last ~30 m tracks a sinking $v_z$ into about $-1.2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at the gear, then cuts. A hover is $T=W$, $v=0$, $z=\mathrm{const}$. A hoverslam is $T>W$ almost all the way, $v=0$ only at $z=0$.

5. Guidance, navigation, and control

Three different jobs. The trainer collapses navigation to omniscient state, guidance to guidance(), and control to a gimbal/fin command that stepPhysics consumes on the next 1/300 s tick.

5.1 Navigation — where am I?

Official Falcon avionics: three-string computers, GPS, IMUs. A radar altimeter is the obvious way to get height above a heaving deck; it is not in the User’s Guide data sheet.

Not in the 2D trainer. No IMU, no GPS noise, no Kalman filter. guidance() reads s directly. The honest upgrade is an estimator on $(x,z,v_x,v_z,\theta,\omega)$ with noisy IMU and occasional altimeter updates.

5.2 Guidance — where should I go?

The papers to actually read are the JPL / Caltech powered-descent sequence (Açıkmeşe, Ploen, Blackmore, Scharf, Carson — see Sources). The structural fact they convexify is the thrust annulus

$$ T_{\min} \le \lVert\mathbf{T}\rVert \le T_{\max}, $$

which is nonconvex. Lossless convexification introduces a slack $\Gamma$ and replaces the annulus with a second-order cone. G-FOLD flew that theory on Masten’s Xombie in 2013. Lars Blackmore later ran Falcon 9 EDL at SpaceX. It is honest to say the theory was sitting in the building. It is not honest to say “Falcon 9 flies G-FOLD.”

Not in the 2D trainer. No SOCP, no annulus, no pointing cone. The guidance ladder is exactly the one the original essay recommended first: $h_\star$ on the vertical channel, a PD on $x$ that commands $\theta$, then an NDI invert for $\delta$.

5.3 Control — make the vehicle do that

  • TVC inner loop. Implemented: $\delta$ tracks $\theta_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ and damps $\omega$ via the NDI invert above. Official first-stage TVC is powered by high-pressure RP-1, not a separate hydraulic fluid.
  • Grid fins. Implemented as a $q$-proportional moment, dead when $q\to 0$. See §6.
  • N2 RCS. Official coast / recovery actuator. Not in the trainer.

6. Aero and grid fins

From entry burn to landing burn the stage is an unpowered reentry vehicle. Mach goes hypersonic → transonic → subsonic. SpaceX’s vehicle page is explicit: the fins “orient the rocket during reentry by moving the center of pressure.”

A Falcon first stage flying engines-first has a heavy aft end. The fins sit at the forward end — which, engines-first, is the trailing end. They pull $x_{\mathrm{cp}}$ aft of the CM so the stage weathercocks engines-into-the-wind.

The essay’s suggested fin model is $M_{\mathrm{fin}}=q S_{\mathrm{ref}} c\,C_m(\alpha,\delta_{\mathrm{fin}})$. What the 2D sim actually adds — label this as extra clothing on the vacuum pendulum — is quadratic drag plus two moment terms:

$$ D = \tfrac12\rho v^2 C_D A,\qquad M_{\mathrm{aero}} = -q A L\cdot 0.05\sin\alpha + q\cdot 16\cdot \delta_{\mathrm{fin}} $$

$C_D A$ is intentionally small so the gimbal story stays in front. There is no Mach table, no CP walk, no transonic hinge-moment spike. Authority dies with $q$. Do not count on aero in the last second.

7. Propulsion constraints that shape the landing

Merlin 1D is a gas-generator, pintle-injected, LOX/RP-1 engine. Official: 845 kN sea-level, restart yes, throttle yes, TVC from the high-pressure RP-1 circuit. The pintle is the same family as the Apollo LM descent engine. The landing burn is a sea-level burn — that is why the center engine, not MVac, lands the booster.

Observed landings often light 3-to-1: center plus two outboards, then the center alone. Three engines give high $a$ while the vehicle is still heavy; one center engine is the lowest $T$ they can get near the deck.

Not in the 2D trainer. Pintle stability, TEA-TEB, chilldown, 3-to-1, ullage/settling, and engine-out are not modeled. $T$ is u * TMAX on one engine, or zero when fuel $\le 1\,\mathrm{kg}$. Use the constants chip for the numbers the plant does believe, and the throttle chip for the gate.

8. Structures and mechanisms

This is your section in the original essay — loads, paths, free bodies. The trainer does not integrate a crush core. It integrates a rigid body and then applies a gate at contact.

8.1 Airframe and legs

Official architecture: Al-Li tanks, common dome, LOX transfer tube, octaweb, composite interstage, four fins, four legs. Legs are carbon fiber over aluminum honeycomb, helium-deployed, with a replaceable honeycomb crush core that caps the load into the octaweb. They latch. They are not a servoed oleo.

In the sim the legs are a visual legsDeploy blend that starts when feetZ is under 450 m or the landing burn is already on. No stroke, no fuse, no one-leg-first hit.

8.2 Free-body of a leg at touchdown

The essay’s tip-over moment about the first contact pad is

$$ M_{\mathrm{tip}} = mg\cdot x_{\mathrm{cm}} + m\dot v_x\cdot h_{\mathrm{cm}}, \qquad x_{\mathrm{cm}}\approx h_{\mathrm{cm}}\theta + x_{\mathrm{pad}}. $$

Not in the 2D trainer. That free-body is not computed. Contact is feetZ(s) ≤ 0, the COM is snapped to the deck, and a gate decides LZ-1 or RUD: $|v_z|\le 4$, $|\theta|\le 9°$, $|x|\le 24\,\mathrm{m}$, $|v_x|\le 3$, $|\omega|\le 40°/\mathrm{s}$.

$$ z_{\mathrm{feet}} = z - \ell\cos\theta - L_{\mathrm{leg}} $$

9. Fuel, margins, and the tyranny of the last 30 seconds

The rocket equation does not care that you are close to home:

$$ \Delta v = I_{\mathrm{sp}}\,g_0\ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}. $$

The sim never evaluates that logarithm. It integrates s.m -= T/(ISP*G0)*dt every substep. Same physics; the closed form is what you use on a whiteboard for a 300 m/s burn (~12% of landing mass at 282 s).

Unofficial Merlin SL $I_{\mathrm{sp}}\approx 282\,\mathrm{s}$ — and that is the number in the const block. A one-second argument with the vertical channel is $T/(I_{\mathrm{sp}}g_0)\approx 0.3\,\mathrm{t/s}$ at full throttle. Fuel is time. Differentiate $h_\star$:

$$ \mathrm{d}h_\star = \frac{V}{a-g}\,\mathrm{d}V. $$

Every extra 10 m/s of residual speed at the fin handoff is tens of meters of ignition altitude. Hard mode in spawnIC cuts the landing load to 5000 kg and widens the box — the intended “fuel-limited” feel.

What the trainer gets right: inverted-pendulum poles; gimbal as a coupled translator-and-rotator; $T/W\gg 1$ and the existence of $h_\star$; a commanded tilt that puts $T$ under the CM; fuel as a clock.

What it lies about (and when the original essay said to add each lie back): no roll/yaw; tiny aero; no slosh; rigid; no wind; instant engine; perfect state; fixed pad; point-mass fuel. Map onto the trainer in that order. If you add slosh before the broomstick poles are visible, you will tune slosh and miss the plant.

A Falcon 9 landing is not magic and it is not a PID meme. It is a rigid body with the mass on the wrong end of the stick, an engine that cannot whisper, a set of fins that work until they don’t, and a guidance problem whose convexification is one of the cleaner pieces of applied optimal control of the last twenty years. Your sim only has to tell that story in a plane. The chips are so you can see the story in the file.
